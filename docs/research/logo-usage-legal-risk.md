# Airline and Airport Logo Usage: U.S. Legal Risk

**Last verified:** 2026-07-17
**Scope:** A U.S.-launched consumer flight-tracking app displaying airline and airport identities.
**Status:** Research summary for product planning, not legal advice. A lawyer should review the actual screens, marks, asset sources, contracts, launch states, and distribution territories.

## Bottom Line

Displaying an airline or airport logo without a license is **not automatically unlawful**, but it can create trademark, false-endorsement, dilution, copyright, contract, and app-store review risk. The outcome is contextual rather than determined by a simple “logos always require permission” rule.

Plain-text airline or airport names used only to identify the operator or location have a stronger nominative-use rationale. Exact logos are harder to justify when the same fact can be communicated with a name, IATA/ICAO code, flight number, or Cirrava-owned generic icon. In the Ninth Circuit, the court has specifically treated stylized marks and logos as potentially “more use of the mark than necessary” where words were adequate, because the visual branding could imply an authorized affiliation. See [*Toyota Motor Sales, U.S.A., Inc. v. Tabari*, 610 F.3d 1171, 1181–82 (9th Cir. 2010)](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2010/07/08/07-55344.pdf).

## Separate Legal and Contract Questions

### 1. Trademark infringement and likelihood of confusion

A logo may be a trademark because a word, symbol, design, or combination can identify the source of goods or services. Trademark ownership is not ownership of a symbol for every purpose; similarity of the marks, the relationship between the parties’ services, and the overall commercial impression are relevant, so presentation matters. See the USPTO explanations of [what a trademark protects](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/basics/what-trademark) and [likelihood of confusion](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/search/likelihood-confusion).

For a registered mark, federal infringement liability turns on use in commerce that is likely to cause confusion, mistake, or deception—not merely the absence of permission. [15 U.S.C. § 1114(1)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title15-section1114). For registered and unregistered marks, [15 U.S.C. § 1125(a)(1)(A)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title15-section1125) also reaches likely confusion about affiliation, association, origin, sponsorship, or approval.

For Cirrava, the strongest risk signals would be using third-party logos in the app icon, product name, splash screen, store artwork, or co-branding; giving them the same prominence as Cirrava’s own identity; using phrases such as “official,” “partner,” or “powered by” without authorization; or otherwise making the experience resemble an airline’s or airport’s own app. A clear independent Cirrava identity and accurate, subordinate text references reduce—but do not automatically eliminate—confusion risk.

### 2. Nominative fair use

Nominative use means using another party’s mark to identify that party or its actual product or service. Under the Ninth Circuit’s test, the referenced product must not be readily identifiable without the mark, only so much of the mark as reasonably necessary may be used, and the user must do nothing that suggests sponsorship or endorsement. Commercial use can still qualify. See the court’s application of the test in [*Toyota Motor Sales, U.S.A., Inc. v. Tabari*, 610 F.3d 1171, 1175–82 (9th Cir. 2010)](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2010/07/08/07-55344.pdf).

This supports truthful text such as “Delta Air Lines flight DL123” or “arriving at San Francisco International Airport (SFO),” provided the overall screen accurately communicates Cirrava’s independent role. It is a weaker basis for reproducing an airline’s distinctive emblem or stylized wordmark when plain text communicates the same information. The Ninth Circuit has expressly contrasted permissible use of a brand name with unnecessary distinctive lettering, fonts, symbols, and logos. See [*Applied Underwriters, Inc. v. Lichtenegger*, 913 F.3d 884, 896–98 (9th Cir. 2019)](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2019/01/15/17-16815.pdf).

The doctrine is not applied identically nationwide. For example, the Third Circuit separates likely-confusion analysis from a three-part nominative-fair-use defense, while the Ninth Circuit uses its nominative-use test to evaluate confusion. Compare [*Century 21 Real Estate Corp. v. LendingTree, Inc.*, 425 F.3d 211, 222–32 (3d Cir. 2005)](https://www2.ca3.uscourts.gov/opinarch/034700p.pdf) with [*Toyota v. Tabari*](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2010/07/08/07-55344.pdf). Launch location, user location, and the forum in which a dispute is brought can therefore matter.

### 3. False endorsement

“False endorsement” is usually a theory under the same Lanham Act provision that prohibits likely confusion about affiliation, sponsorship, or approval. [15 U.S.C. § 1125(a)(1)(A)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title15-section1125). An accurate flight card can still create endorsement risk if its visual treatment makes users think the airline or airport supplied, approved, or operates Cirrava.

A disclaimer such as “Cirrava is an independent service and is not affiliated with or endorsed by any airline or airport” may help make the relationship accurate, but it is not a universal cure for an otherwise misleading design. *Toyota* explains that a disclaimer is not required in every nominative-use case, but may be necessary when other factors suggest sponsorship or endorsement. [*Toyota v. Tabari*, 610 F.3d at 1177–79](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2010/07/08/07-55344.pdf).

### 4. Dilution

Owners of marks that are widely recognized by the U.S. general consuming public can seek an injunction for likely dilution by blurring or tarnishment even without likely confusion or competition. [15 U.S.C. § 1125(c)(1)–(2)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title15-section1125). The statute excludes qualifying nominative or descriptive fair use when the famous mark is not used as a source designation for the user’s own goods or services, as well as news reporting, commentary, and noncommercial use. [15 U.S.C. § 1125(c)(3)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title15-section1125).

That makes use of an airline logo as Cirrava’s own badge, app identity, or decorative brand theme more problematic than a restrained identifier next to the airline’s actual flight. The Supreme Court has confirmed that when another’s mark is used as a source identifier for the defendant’s own product, ordinary trademark infringement and dilution rules apply. [*Jack Daniel’s Properties, Inc. v. VIP Products LLC*, 599 U.S. 140, 156–64 (2023)](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/599us1r35_7m58.pdf).

### 5. Copyright

Trademark and copyright are independent. The Copyright Office says names, titles, slogans, short phrases, typeface, and familiar symbols generally are not copyrightable, but sufficiently original logo artwork may be. See the [Copyright Office logo FAQ](https://www.copyright.gov/help/faq/faq-protect.html) and [Circular 33, *Works Not Protected by Copyright*](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ33.pdf).

If a logo contains protectable artwork, copying it into the app and showing it to users implicates the copyright owner’s reproduction and public-display rights. [17 U.S.C. § 106](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title17-section106). Copyright fair use is a separate, fact-specific analysis of purpose and character, nature of the work, amount used, and market effect. [17 U.S.C. § 107](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title17-section107). A conclusion that a use is nominative under trademark law does not itself resolve copyright, and repeated commercial display of an entire creative logo should not be assumed to be copyright fair use.

Airport ownership does not make a logo automatically public domain. The federal-government exclusion applies to works prepared by U.S. federal officers or employees as part of their official duties; it does not categorically cover city, county, state, port-authority, or privately operated airport branding. See the definition in [17 U.S.C. § 101](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title17-section101) and the federal-work rule in [17 U.S.C. § 105](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title17-section105).

### 6. Brand terms, asset-source contracts, and provider licenses

Even when trademark law would permit a particular nominative reference, the source used to obtain a logo or data can impose separate contractual limits. For example, American Airlines’ current platform terms restrict commercial reuse of platform content and trademarks, deny an implied IP license, and specifically address commercial travel-information services. See [American Airlines Platform Terms §§ 2, 7, 16, and 18](https://www.aa.com/i18n/customer-service/support/legal-information.jsp). Delta publishes logo files and detailed brand rules for media professionals, but its public page’s availability should not be treated as a blanket license for an unrelated commercial app; it directs usage questions to corporate communications. See [Delta’s official logo and brand guidelines](https://news.delta.com/delta-air-lines-logos-and-brand-guidelines). SFO likewise offers downloadable assets and a brand contact, but the download page does not on its face grant Cirrava commercial-app rights. See [SFO’s official brand page](https://www.flysfo.com/es/node/15486).

Cirrava’s proposed flight-data license is also separate from airline and airport logo rights. FlightAware’s public AeroAPI Standard license permits embedded data in a consumer flight-tracking app but grants no other copyright or proprietary rights and bars use that may infringe third parties. It should not be read as an airline- or airport-logo license unless a signed agreement expressly says so. See the [FlightAware AeroAPI Standard License](https://www.flightaware.com/commercial/aeroapi/AeroAPI_Standard_License.pdf).

Maintain an asset register showing, for every third-party logo: the owner, exact version, source file, permission or license, permitted surfaces and territories, required attribution and brand treatment, expiration/termination rules, and a fallback asset. A publicly accessible image or brand guide is not by itself proof of broad commercial permission.

### 7. App-store rules

Platform review can be stricter or more operationally conservative than the legal merits of a fair-use defense:

- Apple says apps should contain content the developer created or licensed, prohibits protected third-party trademarks and copyrighted works without permission, can request authorization, and separately requires permission under a third-party service’s terms. See [App Review Guidelines 5.2.1–5.2.2](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/).
- Google Play prohibits IP infringement, warns that developers may have to prove rights to third-party content, recommends advance submission of written permission for brand names, logos, and graphic assets, and prohibits misleading impersonation or implied authorization. See Google Play’s [Intellectual Property policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9888072?hl=en) and [Impersonation policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9888374?hl=en).

Therefore, a logo use that counsel believes is defensible under U.S. trademark law could still delay review or be removed after a rights-holder complaint if Cirrava cannot produce documentation acceptable to the store.

## Practical Risk Ranking

| Proposed use | Relative launch risk | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Airline/airport name, flight number, and code in ordinary Cirrava typography | Lower, not zero | Stronger identification need; avoids copying distinctive visual branding |
| Cirrava-owned generic plane/terminal icon next to the name or code | Lower | No third-party logo asset; preserves clear Cirrava authorship |
| Exact logo inside a flight card | Medium to high without written rights | Logo may be more than necessary; copyright, endorsement, contract, and store-review issues remain |
| Third-party logo in the Cirrava app icon, splash screen, store listing, or co-branded header | Highest | Most likely to act as source or partnership branding and to trigger store scrutiny |

These rankings are product-risk inferences, not legal determinations about any specific mark.

## V1 Recommendation

Launch v1 without airline or airport logos unless Cirrava has written, traceable rights that cover the exact in-app and store-listing uses. Use accurate airline and airport names, flight numbers, and IATA/ICAO codes in Cirrava’s typography, paired with Cirrava-owned generic iconography. Keep third-party names subordinate to Cirrava’s identity, avoid “official,” “partner,” or equivalent language without authorization, and include a concise independence statement in an appropriate legal/about surface.

Treat logo support as a later licensed feature behind an asset registry and remote kill switch so a disputed or expired asset can be removed without a binary release. Obtain written confirmation from the flight-data provider that its agreement covers every supplied brand asset; a data-display license alone is not enough.

## Questions for Counsel Before Any Logo Launch

1. For the actual screens and launch states, which jurisdiction’s nominative-use and confusion tests are most likely to apply, and do the proposed plain-text name/code references need any changes?
2. Would any proposed exact logo use be considered more than reasonably necessary, a source designation for Cirrava, or likely to suggest airline/airport sponsorship?
3. Is the planned independence disclaimer appropriately worded and placed, and does the overall visual hierarchy undermine it?
4. For each proposed logo, who owns the trademark and any copyright, is the artwork copyrightable, and does Cirrava have permission from every relevant owner or licensor for app, notification, widget, Live Activity, website, screenshot, and store-marketing use?
5. Do any airport logos incorporate government seals or symbols subject to additional state or local restrictions?
6. Do the airline/airport website terms, provider agreement, logo-asset source, or brand guidelines create obligations beyond trademark and copyright law?
7. What documentation should Cirrava retain and submit to Apple or Google, and what takedown/escalation process should it follow if a rights holder objects?
8. Does a U.S. analysis suffice for every intended App Store and Google Play territory, or must foreign trademark, copyright, and unfair-competition rules be cleared before availability there?
